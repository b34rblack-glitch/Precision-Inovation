What this app is

Precision Innovation is three tools that feed each other:

  1. Rifles — the firearm and the optic on top of it.
  2. Loads — the ammunition, recorded like a recipe, with full version history.
  3. Range — what actually happened when you shot that load through that rifle, which becomes a range card you can dial from.

Most reloading apps stop at the second one. They will happily store a hundred load recipes and never once tell you where to hold at 700 yards. Most ballistic apps stop at a solver: they will compute a beautiful trajectory from numbers you typed in, and never learn anything from the fact that you missed.

The point of this app is the loop between them. A load's ballistic data produces a predicted card. You shoot, and log what you actually dialled. Those confirmed holds override the prediction where you have them — and, once you have enough of them, they calibrate the solution so the distances you haven't shot get better too.

The one thing to understand first

Almost every field in this app is optional. Rifles need a name. Loads need a name. That is the entire required set.

This is deliberate. Apps in this category tend to demand forty fields before they let you save anything, which means you either fabricate numbers or give up. Here, every field you fill in makes some calculation more honest, and every field you skip falls back to a sane default or simply switches its feature off. Nothing nags you.

The trade-off is that it is not always obvious what a blank field costs you. That is what this guide is for.

The shortest useful path

If you want a working range card in ten minutes:

  1. Rifles → +. Name it. Set Sight height over bore (measure it — see below), Zero distance, and Turret unit to match your scope. Save.
  2. Loads → +. Name it. Open Ingredients and set BC value, BC model, and Bullet weight. If you know your muzzle velocity, open 6 · QC & results and enter it. Assign the load to the rifle. Save.
  3. Range → your rifle's card. You have a predicted card. Set the start, end and interval distances to whatever your range actually offers.
  4. Shoot. Tap a row on the card, enter what you really dialled, mark it confirmed.
  5. Once you have two or three confirmed holds past 300 yd, tap True-up.

Everything else in this guide is detail on top of that loop.

The four inputs that matter most

If you get nothing else right, get these right.

Two things this app deliberately does not do

It ships no load data. The bullet, powder, primer, brass, die and press pick-lists contain product names and — for bullets — published ballistic coefficients. They contain no charge weights and no recipes, and they never will. Published BCs are physical constants you can verify. A charge weight is a safety decision that belongs to you, your manual, your components, and your rifle. Work up your own loads from current manufacturer data.

It does not sync or phone home. Everything lives in a SQLite database on the device. There is no account and no server. That also means an uninstall takes your data with it, so use Settings → Export if the data matters to you.

Precision Innovation — App Shell, Rifles, and Backup

This section covers how the app is laid out, everything on the Rifles tab, and the Settings screen where you export and restore your data.

A note on how the app is built: all of your data lives on the phone, in a local database. Nothing syncs, nothing uploads, and there is no account. That makes the backup screen the single most important screen in the app — read that section.

Another structural point that matters throughout: there are no app-wide unit settings. MIL/MOA and yards/metres are properties of each rifle, set on the rifle form. Two rifles in the same app can print range cards in different units.

What archiving actually does, in plain terms:

  • The rifle disappears from the Rifles list and from rifle pickers.
  • Nothing is deleted. Loads, workups, range sessions, DOPE entries, and range cards that reference the rifle stay in the database intact, and archived rifles are still included in exported backups.

What the rifle fields actually drive

This is the part worth understanding, because a wrong number here silently poisons every range card the rifle produces.

Sight height over bore

The solver starts the bullet at minus the sight height relative to the line of sight — the bullet begins below your optical axis by exactly this distance and has to climb to cross it. That start offset goes into the zero-angle solve, so it changes the launch angle, and from there it changes every drop figure and every elevation hold on the card.

Practically: sight height matters most at close range, where the bullet is still below or just crossing the line of sight, and its effect shrinks with distance as gravity dominates. Getting it wrong by half an inch will visibly move your near holds and your second (far) zero crossing. Measure from the centre of the scope tube to the centre of the bore. The 1.9 in default is a reasonable figure for a modern precision rifle with a 34 mm tube and tall rings, not a universal truth.

Zero distance

The solver runs a small search for the launch angle that puts the bullet exactly on the line of sight at your zero distance, then computes the whole trajectory from that angle. Every elevation number on the range card is a come-up from that zero.

Two consequences:

  • Zero distance is stored in the rifle's own distance unit, not converted behind your back. A metres rifle keeps 91 m as 91 m; the solver converts to yards internally when it runs.
  • A zero of zero or below is physically meaningless and the solver cannot converge on it. The form blocks it, but if a rifle somehow ends up with one, the range card screen refuses to build and reports:

This rifle has an invalid zero distance. Set a zero greater than 0 to build a range card.

Twist rate → stability → spin drift and aerodynamic jump

Twist rate is text, and the app is liberal about the format: 1:8, 1-8, 1/8, or a bare 8 all parse to 8 inches per turn, and decimals like 1:7.5 work. Anything it cannot parse is treated as no twist rate — silently, with no warning.

When it does parse, twist feeds the Miller gyroscopic stability formula with Litz's velocity and atmosphere corrections:

Sg = 30·m / (t²·d³·l·(1 + l²))  ×  (mv/2800)^(1/3)  ×  (ρ_std / ρ)

where m is bullet weight in grains, d is bullet diameter in inches, t is twist expressed in calibers, and l is bullet length in calibers. The velocity term corrects Miller's 2800 fps assumption; the density term corrects his sea-level-standard air — thinner air stabilises the bullet better, so Sg rises with altitude.

That stability factor then drives two separate corrections on the range card:

  1. Spin drift — a gyroscopically stable bullet drifts sideways as it flies, in the direction of the rifling. The app uses the Litz approximation drift (inches) = 1.25 · (Sg + 1.2) · time-of-flight^1.83, applied per row. This appears as a DRIFT column with an L/R hold direction, and only prints when it would actually move an impact.
  2. Aerodynamic jump — a crosswind tips a spinning bullet, and the bullet's response is a vertical deflection, not a horizontal one. The app uses AJ (MOA per mph) = 0.01·Sg − 0.0024·l + 0.032, and folds the result straight into the elevation hold rather than showing it separately. With a right-hand twist, wind from the left jumps the impact down; wind from the right jumps it up.

Three conditions must all be true before either correction is applied:

  • The rifle has a parseable twist rate.
  • The load's bullet has a length, a diameter, and a weight recorded — Miller needs all three. A load with only a BC is not enough.
  • The range card has spin drift enabled (it is on by default).

Miss any one of those and the app simply computes without them; there is no error message. Aerodynamic jump additionally requires a logged crosswind on the card.

Twist direction sits directly under the twist rate as a Right/Left control, and it matters more than its size suggests. Spin drift and aerodynamic jump both reverse sign with the direction the rifling turns: a right-hand barrel walks the bullet right, a left-hand barrel walks it left by the same amount. At 1000 yd that is roughly 10 inches either way, so getting it wrong puts you about 20 inches off in windage.

The control defaults to Right, which is correct for very nearly every factory barrel. Leave it alone unless you know you have a left-twist barrel. Rifles you created before this setting existed read back as right-hand, so nothing you already saved changes behaviour.

Turret units (MIL / MOA)

This setting decides the angular unit for every hold associated with the rifle:

  • Predicted elevation and wind holds on the range card are converted into this unit.
  • Holds are formatted to one decimal for MIL, two for MOA. The app does not snap holds to turret clicks. A MIL card happens to land on 0.1 steps because that is what one decimal gives you, but an MOA card shows values like 3.13 rather than the nearest quarter-MOA click — round to your own turret when you dial.
  • The big-type field card (the black-and-amber screen you use at the bench) prints in this unit, and labels itself with it.
  • Exported/printed range cards use it.
  • Both truing routines — solving muzzle velocity from your confirmed holds, and solving a drag scale factor — compare predictions against observations in this unit.

Important: changing turret units does not rewrite your logged DOPE. Every DOPE entry stores the unit you actually dialled alongside the number, and the app converts on display. Switching a rifle from MIL to MOA re-expresses your old confirmations, it does not corrupt them.

Distance unit (yd / m)

This is a display and grid setting, not a storage unit — range card distances and DOPE distances are stored canonically in yards regardless:

  • Range card rows and field card rows are converted for display, and the column header shows yd or m.
  • The Zero distance field suffix, the range card's distance settings dialog, and the quick-DOPE entry dialog all switch to this unit.
  • The card's preset distance grids are generated so that a metres rifle gets rows at round metre marks — 100 m, 150 m, 200 m — rather than the ugly conversions you would get from a yard grid. (The Bench preset runs 100–1000 in 25-unit steps; Hunting runs 100–500 in 50-unit steps. The preset sets the distance grid only — start, end and step. It does not change which columns appear; the card shows the same columns either way.)

Your data

The first card explains the situation:

Everything lives on this device — nothing is uploaded anywhere. Export a backup before switching phones, and back up regularly.

Take this literally. There is no cloud copy. If the phone is lost, wiped, or the app is uninstalled, an exported backup file is the only way your data survives.

Export Backup

Tapping Export Backup dumps the entire database to a JSON file named precision-backup-YYYY-MM-DD.json and hands it to your system share sheet (titled Export backup), from where you can save it to files, cloud storage, email, or wherever you like. The button shows a spinner while it works, and Import Backup is disabled during the export.

The export includes every table in the app: rifles (including archived ones), loads, load versions, workups, workup steps, range sessions, chronograph strings, individual shots, DOPE entries, and range card configurations. Photos are not embedded — the file stores the reference to the photo on the device, so photos do not travel with the backup to a new phone.

If anything goes wrong you get an alert titled Export failed with the reason.

Import Backup (destructive)

Tapping Import Backup opens your device file picker, filtered to JSON files. Backing out of the picker cancels quietly with no further prompt.

Once you pick a file, you must confirm:

Replace all data?

Importing a backup replaces everything currently in the app. A safety copy of current data is saved first.

[ Cancel ] [ Replace ]

Replace is a destructive action and it means what it says: this is a replace, not a merge. Everything currently in the app is deleted and the file's contents are loaded in its place. There is no way to combine two backups.

What happens when you tap Replace:

  1. The app writes a safety copy of your current data to precision-pre-restore-backup.json in the app's cache directory, before touching anything.
  2. Every table is emptied and reloaded from the file inside a single database transaction — so if the import fails part-way, the database rolls back to exactly where it was rather than being left half-wiped.
  3. On success you get:

Backup restored

Imported N rifles, N loads, N sessions.

A caution about that safety copy: it is written to the app's cache directory, which the operating system may clear at any time, and it is never handed to the share sheet — you cannot see or open it from Settings. It protects you against a bad import file in the moment; it is not a substitute for exporting a real backup first.

How versioning works, and why it matters

This is the single most important behaviour on this screen, so it is worth being precise.

A load has a name, cartridge and rifle assignment that belong to the load itself, and one or more versions, each of which holds a complete recipe — components, all six process stages, and the velocity data. One version is the current one; that's what the detail screen shows and what range cards are built from.

When you save an edit, the app applies a copy-on-write rule:

  • If the current version is already referenced — because a range session was logged against it, or because a workup was started from it (or produced it) — your edits are saved as a brand-new version, numbered one higher, with the old version kept intact as its parent. The new version becomes current. You get a confirmation: "New version created — This load had range history, so your changes were saved as a new version. Past results stay tied to the recipe that produced them."
  • If nothing references it yet — a load you just created and are still tinkering with — the edit is written straight into the existing version. No new version, no clutter.

Why this matters at the bench: your groups, your chronograph strings and your DOPE are all attached to a specific version, not to the load in general. If bumping your charge from 41.3 to 41.5 quietly overwrote the recipe, last month's 0.4 MOA group would now claim to have been shot with a load that never fired it. The copy-on-write rule means recorded history can never be retroactively rewritten — old results stay bolted to the exact recipe that produced them, and the version history card lets you look back at what that recipe was.

Two practical consequences:

  1. Once you start a workup on a load, that load's base version is locked. The next edit will spin off a new version even if you only changed one number.
  2. Every save from the edit screen goes through the same check — so if the current version is referenced, even a change that only touched the load's name results in a new version being written. If you are only renaming, expect the version counter to tick.

Which fields actually change your come-ups

Out of everything above, exactly seven pieces of data reach the trajectory solver:

  1. BC value — the drag number.
  2. BC model (G7 or G1) — which curve that number belongs to.
  3. Velocity-banded BCs (optional) — used in place of the single BC when present.
  4. Bullet weight — energy figures, and a prerequisite for spin drift.
  5. Bullet length + diameter — spin drift, in combination with the rifle's twist rate.
  6. Muzzle velocity (avg) — the starting speed.
  7. MV measured at + Temp sensitivity — the powder-temperature correction to that speed.

Everything else on this form is record-keeping. Your charge weight, brass, primer, bushing size, shoulder bump, seating depth, runout — none of it is fed into the ballistic solution. It exists so that when a load shoots well you can reproduce it exactly, and when it stops shooting well you can see what changed. (The charge weight has one extra job: it seeds the starting charge when you begin a workup.)

Number entry rules

Every numeric field behaves the same way: leave it blank and it is simply not recorded; type something that isn't a number and the save is blocked with "Enter a number like 41.5" next to that field. Decimal commas are accepted as well as decimal points, so a European keyboard works fine. Fields that describe a physical dimension must be greater than zero; fields where zero is a genuine reading (shoulder bump, primer seating depth, charge tolerance, runout) accept zero but reject negatives; and the three signed fields (jump to lands, MV measured at, and point-of-impact in workups) accept a minus sign.

A workup is a structured load-development test: a series of charge weights, shot in your rifle, with the results recorded charge by charge. How the charge series is generated. The arithmetic is deliberately simple and completely predictable: the nth charge is

charge(n) = start charge + (n − 1) × increment

rounded to two decimal places. That rounding is there purely to stop floating-point noise from turning 41.3 into 41.300000000000004; it does not change the numbers you asked for. So a start of 41.0 with a 0.3 increment over 10 charges gives 41.0, 41.3, 41.6, 41.9, 42.2, 42.5, 42.8, 43.1, 43.4, 43.7. No series is generated at all if the number of charges is zero or less, the increment is negative, or the start charge is zero or less.

How average, SD and ES are calculated

When you save a string of velocities, the app computes and stores three numbers from the individual shots:

  • Average — the plain arithmetic mean of the velocities.
  • SD — the sample standard deviation, using the n − 1 (Bessel-corrected) divisor, not the population formula. With only one shot in the string no SD is recorded at all.
  • ES — extreme spread: the fastest shot minus the slowest.

The n − 1 choice is the right one for what you are doing: your five-shot string is a sample drawn from the much larger population of rounds that load could produce, and you want an estimate of that whole population's spread. The population formula (dividing by n) would systematically under-report your SD — flatteringly so, and most misleadingly on exactly the small strings handloaders shoot. Expect this app's SD to read very slightly higher than a chronograph or app that divides by n; on a 5-shot string the difference is around 12%.

Each charge card displays these as 2704 fps SD 6.2 ES 15 — average rounded to whole fps, SD to one decimal, ES to whole fps.

The velocity chart and flat-spot detection

Once three or more charges have velocity data, a Velocity vs charge card appears above the step cards, plotting average velocity (vertical, with the minimum and maximum labelled) against charge weight (horizontal, labelled on every other point). The points are joined by a line so a plateau is visible as a flattening in the curve.

The app then looks for flat spots automatically. It works out the jump in average velocity between each pair of neighbouring charges, takes the median of those jumps as "what a normal step looks like for this ladder", and flags any run where each jump is no more than 40% of that median. A run has to span at least three consecutive charges to count. Each qualifying window is drawn with its points enlarged and highlighted in the accent colour, and listed underneath as "Flat spot around 42.2 gr" with the velocity spread across that window ("9 fps spread across the window"), plus a Promote button. The suggested charge is the middle charge of the window.

Because the test is relative to your own ladder's median step, it adapts to your cartridge — a big magnum with 30 fps steps and a .223 with 8 fps steps are judged on the same terms. If velocities are perfectly uniform across the whole ladder, no flat spot is reported. When nothing qualifies you get: "No velocity flat spots detected yet — keep filling in charges, or pick a winner manually below."

There is no chart for group size or point of impact — for OCW and ladder tests those are read off the step cards themselves (0.75" group, POI 0.3" up, -0.5" right), which is how you spot the charge window where impact stays put.

Picking a winner and promoting it

You can promote a charge in two ways: the Promote button next to a detected flat spot, or the Pick a winner manually chips near the bottom, which list every charge in the series. Either way you confirm: "Promote 42.2 gr? — This becomes the new current version of the load, ready for confirmation and DOPE." Afterwards the app offers "Confirm it at the range when you can" with a shortcut straight into a new range session.

What promotion actually does. It creates a brand-new load version, numbered one above the load's highest existing version, whose parent is the version the workup was based on. That new version becomes the load's current version, and the workup is marked complete and linked to the version it produced.

Deliberately left blank — MV measured at (mvTempRefF). This is the one that looks like an oversight and isn't. The muzzle velocity on the new version is the number your chronograph recorded during the workup, and the app has no idea what the powder temperature was on that day — you never told it. If it copied the reference temperature across from the old version, it would be stamping "measured at 70 °F" onto a velocity that might have been shot at 95 °F, and every subsequent temperature correction would then be computed off a false baseline, pushing your come-ups in the wrong direction. Blank is honest: with no reference temperature the app simply applies no temperature adjustment and uses your measured velocity as-is, which is exactly right until you tell it more. When you know what the temperature was, edit the load and fill in MV measured at.

Also not carried over: all of your process detail. The new version starts with stages 1 through 6 empty — case prep, sizing and dies, priming lot and depth, charge method and tolerance, seating die, micrometer setting, jump, neck tension, bullet lot, runout, loaded weight and the notes fields are all blank on the promoted version, and the Yes/No prep flags reset to No. A promotion is a statement about a charge weight, not a record of how you assembled that particular batch, so nothing about your process is asserted on your behalf. If your process was the same, copy it forward yourself with the pencil button; if it wasn't, you're starting from a clean sheet anyway.

Finishing up

Mark Complete (shown while a workup is anything other than complete) sets its status to complete without promoting a charge — useful for a test that told you what you needed to know but produced no winner. Promoting a charge marks the workup complete automatically.

Archive Workup hides the workup from the load screen: "Archive workup? — It will be hidden from this load, but its data is kept." Nothing is deleted, and the load version the workup referenced stays locked, so your history remains intact.

The Range Tab — Sessions, DOPE, the Range Card and Truing

Everything in this section lives under the Range tab. The idea behind it is simple: the app predicts a firing solution from your load data, you shoot, and whatever you actually confirmed on steel or paper permanently overrides the prediction at that distance. Everything in Conditions is optional. Every field you fill in makes the predicted card more honest; every field you leave blank falls back to a standard value. How the atmosphere is assembled. The card looks for the newest session that recorded at least one of temperature, pressure or altitude. From that session:

  • Temperature is used as entered; if it is blank the card uses 59 °F.
  • Pressure is used as entered. If pressure is blank but altitude is present, the app computes the standard-atmosphere pressure for that altitude instead. If both are blank it uses 29.9213 inHg (ICAO sea level).
  • Humidity is used as entered, otherwise treated as 0%.

Air density is then computed properly as a mixture of dry air and water vapour, and the speed of sound is derived from that same air. That speed of sound is what produces the transonic (‡) and subsonic (‡‡) marks on the card — enter a cold, dense day and the marks creep closer; enter a hot, thin, high day and they push further out.

Practically: pressure beats altitude. If you have a Kestrel or any weather meter, type the station pressure and leave altitude blank. Altitude alone assumes standard-day pressure for that elevation, which is close but not what your barometer says on a stormy morning.

Humidity is worth less than shooters expect. Moist air is slightly less dense than dry air, so high humidity very slightly flattens the trajectory. It is a real effect and the app models it, but it is far below temperature and pressure in importance. Leave it blank without guilt.

Wind speed and the wind direction clock

Wind is the one condition that does not automatically change the solution. The card is always solved at a 10 mph full-value (90°) crosswind and the wind columns are read as holds per that reference. Your logged wind only enters the picture if you switch Use logged wind on in the card's Advanced sheet, and then it does two things:

  1. The W5 column is replaced by W·LOG, which scales the 10 mph hold by the crosswind component you logged (|crosswind| ÷ 10) and prints the hold direction, L or R.
  2. If spin drift is active for this rifle and bullet, that same crosswind component feeds aerodynamic jump — the small vertical shift a crosswind produces on a spinning bullet — which is folded into the elevation holds.

The crosswind component is taken from the clock direction the wind blows from:

crosswind = − wind speed × sin(clock × 30°)

So 3 o'clock (wind from your right) gives a negative value and you hold right; 9 o'clock gives a positive value and you hold left; 12 and 6 o'clock are pure head/tail winds and produce essentially zero crosswind.

You set the direction on the New Session screen, in the Conditions section, using the Wind from (clock) picker: a — chip for "not recorded", then 12, 1, 2 through 11. The label is worth reading twice — it is the direction the wind blows from, which is the way shooters call wind and the opposite of the way weather reports give it.

If you leave it on —: the card falls back to 12 o'clock, and 12 o'clock is a pure headwind — zero crosswind. W·LOG will then read zero no matter what wind speed you logged. If you turn Use logged wind on, set the clock too, or the column tells you nothing.

Powder temperature and muzzle velocity

If — and only if — the load carries both an MV measured at reference temperature and an MV temp sensitivity (fps per °F), and the session carries a temperature, the card shifts the muzzle velocity before solving:

adjusted MV = stated MV + (session temp − reference temp) × sensitivity

That is why a load chronographed at 70 °F and shot at 25 °F drops velocity on the card automatically. When the shift is applied the MV line on the card is tagged (temp adj), and the PDF meta line gains temp-adj MV.

Two rules worth knowing. First, if any of the three values is missing, nothing happens — no guessing. Second, a trued muzzle velocity is never temperature-adjusted again: truing already solved for the day you shot, so adjusting it a second time would double-count the weather.

What "Confirmed" means

This is the most important switch in the app.

  • Confirmed — "this hold put rounds where I wanted them." It becomes truth. The range card replaces its own prediction at that distance with your number, and the number becomes eligible for calibration.
  • Provisional — "this is what I tried, I'm not certain." It is stored, listed and searchable, but the range card ignores it completely and it can never influence calibration.

Entries are tagged CONF in amber or PROV in grey in the list.

Only confirmed entries feed calibration, and the reason is worth stating plainly: truing works by assuming the difference between the prediction and your DOPE is caused by an error in the model, then adjusting the model until the difference disappears. If you feed it a hold you were not sure about — a called flyer, a guessed correction, a shot in switching wind — the solver will faithfully bend your muzzle velocity or your BC to fit that mistake, and it will bend the predictions at every distance as a result. Confirmed status is your signature on the data. Be stingy with it.

To reach the card, a confirmed entry must also live in a session that is not archived and that is logged against the same rifle and the same load. Load lineage is handled for you: editing a load creates a new version, but DOPE logged against older versions of the same load still counts.

Two-stage truing (the True-up button)

Truing is the app making its predictions agree with your rifle. It follows the industry-standard two-stage approach — velocity first, then drag — the same shape as Applied Ballistics' Drag Scale Factor and Hornady 4DOF's axial form factor.

Which knob to turn, and why

Everything the solver does downrange rests on two numbers: how fast the bullet left and how quickly it slows down. When your DOPE at distance disagrees with the prediction, one of those two is wrong — and which one you should adjust depends entirely on whether you actually measured the first one.

No chronograph → true velocity. A stated or manual muzzle velocity is easily 30–60 fps off for your particular barrel. Through the supersonic mid-range, trajectory error is dominated by velocity error, so your drop at 300–600 yards back-solves muzzle velocity cleanly and reliably. Fix the speed and the whole curve, near and far, falls into place. This is why the app defaults to asking.

Chronograph → true drag. If you have measured muzzle velocity, that number is not the problem, and forcing it to absorb a long-range miss actively damages the card. A published BC is an average from a test barrel; your bullet, at your velocity, in your air, may simply shed speed a few percent faster or slower than the G1/G7 reference. Bending MV to fix a 900-yard miss would mis-model the entire curve — you would over-correct at short range in order to fix long range, and end up wrong everywhere. Scaling the BC leaves your measured speed alone and changes only the rate of slowing down, which is exactly where the error lives.

The short version: velocity error shows up early and grows steadily; drag error is nearly invisible up close and grows fast at distance. Adjust the knob that matches where your misses are — and if you know your speed for certain, drag is the only honest option left.

What happens when you press True-up

The button reads "Calibrate velocity or drag from confirmed DOPE" to a screen reader. Its behaviour branches:

If MV came from a chronograph (that is, the load has no stated muzzle velocity and the card is running on a logged velocity string — the MV line reads (chrono)), the app does not ask. It goes straight to drag truing, because the velocity is measured and the residual long-range error must therefore be drag.

Otherwise you get a choice:

How the search works

Both stages use the same machinery. The app takes every confirmed hold beyond the minimum distance, converts each into the rifle's turret unit, and defines the error of a candidate as the sum of the squared differences between what the solver predicts and what you confirmed, evaluated on a 1-yard-resolution trajectory in still air.

It then runs a golden-section search on that error — a bracketing method that repeatedly shrinks the interval using the golden ratio and converges on the minimum without needing derivatives. It assumes the error curve has a single bottom over the search interval, which is true for both velocity and drag over the ranges used here. It stops when the interval falls below the tolerance (1 fps for velocity, 0.001 for the drag scale) or after 30 iterations, whichever comes first.

Crucially, the search solves against exactly the physics the card is showing: the same atmosphere, the same spin drift, Coriolis, incline and banded-BC settings, and the same already-temperature-adjusted muzzle velocity. Whatever the search finds is therefore explaining only what the displayed solution could not — it is not quietly re-absorbing an effect you already switched on.

Practical sequence

  1. Chronograph if you can, or set the manual's velocity if you cannot.
  2. Log confirmed holds at several distances, including at least one past 300 yards and ideally past 400.
  3. Press True-up. Take Velocity if the speed was a guess; take Drag (BC) if it was measured — and note that the app skips the question entirely when it knows the MV is chronograph data.
  4. Check the card. The pred sub-lines under your confirmed rows should now be small or gone.
  5. Shoot further out, confirm more holds, and true again as data accumulates.

Undoing a calibration

Both are reversible, and both live on this card only.

  • Reset MV — "Discard trued velocity? The card goes back to the load's stated muzzle velocity."
  • Reset BC — "Discard drag calibration? The card returns to the published BC."

Do this whenever the underlying situation changes — a new barrel, a rebuilt load, a chronograph session that supersedes a guess — because a calibration solved against old conditions is worse than no calibration at all.

The core model: a point-mass 3-DOF trajectory integrated with RK4

What the app is actually simulating

The app does not look your solution up in a table and it does not evaluate a drop formula. It flies the bullet, one tiny slice of time at a time.

The bullet is treated as a point mass with three degrees of freedom (3-DOF): it has a position and a velocity in three dimensions, and exactly two things act on it — gravity and aerodynamic drag. It is not modelled as a spinning body with its own pitch and yaw attitude (that would be a 6-DOF model; see Limits).

The coordinate frame the solver uses:

AxisDirection
xDownrange, along the line of sight
yVertical, measured relative to the line of sight (negative = below)
zLateral, positive to the right

The bullet starts at x = 0, at y = −sightHeight (it starts below your line of sight, because your scope sits above the bore), with z = 0.

The equation of motion

At every instant the solver computes the acceleration acting on the bullet:

# Velocity of the bullet relative to the moving air mass
rx = vx
ry = vy
rz = vz − windSpeed          # crosswind blows along +z
speed = sqrt(rx² + ry² + rz²)

mach = speed / speedOfSound
Cd   = standardDragCoefficient(model, mach)      # from the G1 or G7 table

# Retardation, m/s², along the air-relative velocity vector
decel = (π/8) · airDensity · speed² · Cd / BC
k     = decel / speed

ax = −k·rx − gAlong
ay = −k·ry − gPerp
az = −k·rz

Three things are worth reading twice:

  1. Drag acts along the bullet's velocity relative to the air, not along the line of sight. This is why the crosswind term shows up inside the drag calculation rather than being bolted on afterwards, and it is why wind deflection comes out correctly without a separate wind formula.
  2. Drag is proportional to speed squared, and the drag coefficient Cd itself changes with Mach number. That is a doubly non-linear relationship, and it is the reason a closed-form drop formula cannot be exact.
  3. gAlong and gPerp are the two components of gravity in the shooting frame. For a level shot they are 0 and 9.80665 m/s². For an inclined shot they are rotated — see Incline.

RK4, and the actual step size

Having an equation for acceleration is not the same as having a trajectory. To get the trajectory you must integrate: start from the muzzle conditions and repeatedly ask "given where the bullet is and how fast it is going right now, where will it be a moment from now?"

The app uses classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) with a fixed time step of 0.001 seconds (1 millisecond):

const DT = 0.001; // s

RK4 does not simply take the acceleration at the start of the step and assume it holds for the whole millisecond. It evaluates the acceleration four times per step — at the beginning, twice at the midpoint using progressively better estimates, and once at the end — then combines them with the classic 1-2-2-1 weighting:

newPosition = position + (dt/6)·(v1 + 2·v2 + 2·v3 + v4)
newVelocity = velocity + (dt/6)·(a1 + 2·a2 + 2·a3 + a4)

That fourth-order weighting is what buys the accuracy. The error of an RK4 step shrinks with the fourth power of the step size: halve the step and the error drops by a factor of sixteen. With a 1 ms step, a 1000 yd .308 shot takes about 1791 steps (time of flight 1.791 s), each step costing four drag evaluations — roughly 7,200 physics evaluations per trajectory. On a phone that is about 2 milliseconds of work for a full 1000 yd card at 25 yd increments. The test suite budgets 250 ms for that solve; the code delivers it in well under 1 % of the budget.

Why integration beats a closed-form approximation

The old "rule of thumb" drop formulas — and the simple vacuum equation

y = x·tanθ − g·x² / (2·v²·cos²θ)

— all assume the bullet's velocity is either constant or decaying in some tidy mathematical way. Real bullets do neither. A .308 leaves at Mach 2.2, and by 1000 yd it is at Mach 0.975. Along the way its drag coefficient climbs about 45 % — from roughly 0.28 at the muzzle to a peak of 0.404 at Mach 1.05 — as it crosses the transonic region, and then falls again. There is no closed-form expression for the path of an object whose drag coefficient is an arbitrary tabulated function of its own instantaneous speed.

Integration sidesteps the problem entirely. Over a single millisecond the bullet's speed barely changes, so treating the drag as locally constant is nearly exact — and RK4 accounts for even that small change. Stack 1,791 of those nearly exact millisecond answers together and you get a trajectory that agrees with an established reference solver to within half an inch at 1000 yards (see Accuracy).

The practical consequence: the app remains accurate in the places where approximations fall apart — past transonic, at steep angles, at altitude, and with unusual BCs — because it is not approximating anything about the shape of the curve.

Getting the numbers onto your card

The integrator marches in time, but your card is printed in distance. The solver watches for the moment the bullet crosses each requested distance, then linearly interpolates between the two bracketing 1 ms steps to produce the exact values at that distance. Your 437-yard row is a real 437-yard answer, not the 440-yard row rounded.

The march stops when it runs out of requested distances, or one metre past the last one, or if the bullet has fallen more than 1000 m below the line of sight, or after 20 seconds of flight — whichever comes first. These are safety rails, not physics; a normal shot never touches them.

Why G7 for modern match bullets, G1 for the rest

A ballistic coefficient only means "this bullet slows down like the reference projectile, scaled." That statement is only true if your bullet is actually shaped like the reference.

  • A modern 175 gr SMK, 6.5 mm 140 gr Hybrid or 6 mm ELD-M is a long, sharp-ogive, boat-tail bullet — geometrically a close relative of the G7 reference. Its G7 BC is close to constant across the useful speed range, so one number describes it well from muzzle to target.
  • Force the same bullet onto G1 and you are asking a boat-tail to decelerate like a flat-base. The single BC you enter will be right at one speed and wrong everywhere else — typically it fits well at short range and progressively under-predicts drop past 600 yd.

Use G1 when that is the only data you have: older load manuals, most factory ammunition boxes, plinking FMJ, and flat-base hunting bullets whose actual shape resembles the G1 reference reasonably well.

Rule of thumb: use the model the BC was published in. If the manufacturer prints a G7 BC, select G7. If it prints G1, select G1.

How the BC scales the drag (the form-factor method)

The app does not compute your bullet's drag from its geometry. It computes the reference projectile's drag from the table, then scales the whole retardation by your ballistic coefficient:

retardation = (π/8) · ρ · v² · Cd_standard(Mach) / BC

where BC is converted to SI mass-per-area before use:

BC_kgm2 = BC_published × bcScale × 703.06958      # 1 lb/in² = 703.06958 kg/m²

Physically, BC is a sectional density divided by a form factor — it is how much mass the bullet has behind each unit of frontal area, relative to the reference shape. Because it sits in the denominator, doubling the BC halves the deceleration at every speed. A bullet with a BC of 1.0 lb/in² in a given model decelerates exactly like that model's reference projectile.

The bcScale factor in the formula is the app's drag-truing knob (1.0 = published BC). It multiplies the effective BC — and every velocity band alike — so you can calibrate the drag model against confirmed DOPE without touching the published number. Values below 1 mean more drag than the model predicts; above 1, less. It is guarded: anything non-finite or ≤ 0 is treated as 1.

Velocity-banded BC

BC is not really a constant

A ballistic coefficient is a fit, not a property. It is the single number that best makes a real bullet's deceleration look like the reference projectile's. But the real bullet's drag curve and the reference's are different shapes, so the best-fit number drifts as the bullet slows — typically the effective BC falls as velocity falls, because bullets become relatively draggier as they approach and cross transonic.

Sierra has published banded BCs for decades for exactly this reason: rather than one number, they list a BC for each velocity band, e.g. 0.243 above 2600 fps, 0.255 from 1800 to 2600 fps, and so on.

What the app does with them

Modern proprietary solutions to the same problem — Doppler-derived custom drag curves, Applied Ballistics' Custom Drag Models, and 4-DOF-style approaches — are closed data sets that cannot be shipped in an open application. Velocity-banded BC is this app's open-data stand-in for those curves. It captures the same physical effect (BC varying with speed) using data that anyone can look up in a Sierra manual and type in.

You enter a list of segments, each being a minimum velocity and a BC. Before the solve, the app normalises that list:

  1. Discards garbage — any segment whose minimum velocity is not a finite number, or whose BC is not a finite positive number.
  2. Sorts descending by minimum velocity, so you can enter them in any order.
  3. Converts to SI, applying bcScale to every band identically.
  4. If nothing survives — an empty list, or all-garbage input — it silently falls back to the single BC you entered. Bad segment data can never break your card.

How the solver picks a band, every millisecond

At each of those ~1,791 integration steps, the solver takes the bullet's current air-relative speed and walks the sorted band list from the top:

for each band (highest minimum velocity first):
    if speed ≥ band.minimumVelocity:  use that band's BC
# below the lowest band: keep using the LOWEST band's BC

That last line matters. The bands do not run out. Once the bullet drops below your slowest listed threshold, the app holds the last (slowest) band's BC for the rest of the flight rather than reverting to anything else.

Two consequences worth knowing:

  • A single segment with a minimum velocity of 0 reproduces a plain single-BC solve exactly, digit for digit.
  • Because the band applies at the bullet's current speed and a bullet is at muzzle velocity only for an instant, the top band often barely gets used. With segments of {≥ 2600 fps → 0.243, ≥ 1800 fps → 0.255} and a 2600 fps muzzle velocity, the bullet is already at 2425 fps by 100 yd, so the 0.255 band governs essentially the entire flight — and below 1800 fps (past ~495 yd) that same 0.255 is held. Set your band thresholds with the velocities the bullet actually spends its flight at, not with the muzzle velocity.

Zeroing

What "zero" means to the solver

A zero is a launch angle. Your barrel is not pointed at the target; it is pointed slightly above your line of sight, so that the bullet's arc crosses that line of sight at your chosen zero distance. Everything on your card is measured relative to that line, so getting the launch angle right is a prerequisite for every other number being right.

The app solves for it numerically with an iterative Newton-style search:

angle = 0.001 rad                      # ~1 mil initial guess
repeat up to 5 times:
    fly the trajectory to the zero distance
    miss = height of the bullet above the line of sight at that distance
    if |miss| < 0.0005 m (0.5 mm):  stop — good enough
    angle = angle + atan2(−miss, zeroDistance)

Each pass flies a complete RK4 trajectory out to the zero distance, measures how far the bullet missed the line of sight vertically, and rotates the barrel by exactly the angle that miss subtends at the zero distance. Because that correction is geometrically the right first-order answer, the search converges in two or three passes; the loop is capped at five and the tolerance is half a millimetre.

Guard: if the zero distance is zero or negative there is no meaningful launch angle, so the solver returns a flat angle of 0 rather than dividing by zero. The range card screen catches this earlier and asks you to fix the rifle's zero.

Why sight height matters

The bullet starts at y = −sightHeight. Your scope sits above your bore, so the bullet begins its flight below your line of sight and must climb to reach it.

This is not a bookkeeping detail — it changes the launch angle, and the launch angle propagates to every row of the card. With a 100 yd zero, raising the sight height from 1.5 in to 2.5 in requires 1 inch more climb over 100 yd, which is an extra 10 inches of rise by 1000 yd, minus the 1 inch lower starting point:

The six advanced effects

All six default to off. With none of their inputs supplied, the solver's output is bit-for-bit identical to the plain trajectory — this is explicitly locked down by the test suite.

Three of the six — spin drift, Coriolis and aerodynamic jump — are applied as closed-form corrections at each output sample rather than being integrated. The other three enter the physics properly. Incline and wind are integrated — incline only rotates gravity, and the crosswind is part of the air-relative velocity the drag term acts on. Powder temperature is not a solver effect at all: it adjusts muzzle velocity before the trajectory is solved.

1. Gyroscopic spin drift

What it is. A spin-stabilised bullet does not fly with its nose exactly along its flight path. As the trajectory arcs downward, the gyroscopic bullet's nose lags slightly above the path and — because a gyroscope responds to a torque 90° away from where it is applied — points slightly to one side. That small persistent yaw generates a small persistent sideways lift. Over a second and a half of flight it adds up. A right-hand twist drifts right; a left-hand twist drifts left. It happens in perfectly still air and has nothing to do with wind.

Step one — the Miller gyroscopic stability factor, Sg:

t  = twistInPerTurn / bulletDiameterIn      # twist in calibres
l  = bulletLengthIn / bulletDiameterIn      # length in calibres

Sg_base = 30·m / ( t² · d³ · l · (1 + l²) )       # m in grains, d in inches

Sg = Sg_base · cbrt(muzzleVelocityFps / 2800) · (1.225 / airDensity)

The first line is Miller's rule. The two correction factors are Litz's: Miller's formula assumes 2800 fps in standard sea-level air (1.225 kg/m³), so the app applies a cube-root velocity correction and a linear density correction. Thinner air destabilises the bullet less, so Sg rises as air density falls — which is why a marginal load that keyholes at sea level can shoot cleanly at altitude.

For the reference .308 (175 gr, 1:11.25, 1.24 in long, 0.308 in diameter, at 2600 fps, ICAO sea level) this yields Sg = 1.897.

Step two — the Litz spin-drift approximation:

spinDrift_inches = ±1.25 · (Sg + 1.2) · timeOfFlight^1.83

+ for a right-hand twist, − for a left-hand twist. Note the exponent of 1.83 on time of flight — drift grows faster than linearly with flight time, which is why it is negligible up close and matters at distance.

Inputs required: barrel twist rate, twist direction, bullet length, bullet diameter, and bullet weight. Without bullet weight the effect is silently skipped, because Sg cannot be computed.

2. Coriolis

What it is. The earth rotates underneath the bullet during its flight. Your rifle, your target and the ground between them are all riding a spinning sphere; the bullet, once it leaves the muzzle, is not attached to it. Over a second or two of flight the ground moves slightly relative to the bullet's inertial path. This is not the bullet being pushed — it is the target arriving somewhere slightly different from where you aimed.

The app uses the earth's sidereal rotation rate:

Ω = 7.292115e-5 rad/s

Horizontal term:

horizontalDrift_metres = Ω · downrangeDistance · timeOfFlight · sin(latitude)

This deflects right in the northern hemisphere and left in the southern, regardless of which way you are facing. It scales with sin(latitude): zero at the equator, maximum at the poles. It is completely azimuth-independent — firing north, south, east or west gives the same horizontal answer.

Vertical term (Eötvös effect):

eotvosFactor = 2 · Ω · muzzleVelocity · cos(latitude) · sin(azimuth) / 9.80665

verticalShift_metres = dropBelowBoreLine · eotvosFactor

Firing east, you are adding your muzzle velocity to the earth's eastward rotation; the bullet's slightly greater angular momentum means effective gravity is slightly reduced, and it shoots high. Firing west it shoots low. The factor is sin(azimuth), so the effect is maximum due east (90°) and due west (270°), and exactly zero due north or south. It scales with cos(latitude): maximum at the equator, zero at the poles — the opposite latitude dependence from the horizontal term.

Note that the code multiplies by the drop below the extended bore line — the purely gravity-driven part of the trajectory — rather than by the path relative to the line of sight. That is the correct decomposition, since the Eötvös effect scales effective gravity, not your sight geometry.

Inputs required: latitude (negative for southern hemisphere) and firing azimuth in degrees clockwise from true north.

3. Incline / angle firing

What it is. Shooting steeply uphill or downhill, you hit high if you use your flat-ground dope. The reason is not that gravity got weaker — it is that gravity no longer pulls perpendicular to your line of sight. Only the component of gravity across the bore line bends the bullet away from where you are aiming; the component along the bore line just speeds it up or slows it down.

What the app does: it decomposes gravity into the shooting frame and integrates the real trajectory:

gPerpendicular = 9.80665 · cos(inclineAngle)     # bends the path away from LOS
gAlongBore     = 9.80665 · sin(inclineAngle)     # uphill (+) decelerates

The x axis stays along the (now inclined) line of sight, so all distances remain slant ranges — the distance your rangefinder reads, not the horizontal map distance.

One subtlety handled correctly: the zero angle is solved with flat gravity even when the shot is inclined. Your rifle was zeroed on level ground, and tilting the rifle does not re-zero it.

Why the "rifleman's rule" cosine shortcut is only approximate. The classic field expedient — multiply your range by the cosine of the angle, or multiply your flat-ground drop by the cosine — assumes the only thing the angle changes is the perpendicular gravity component. That would be exactly true in a vacuum. In real air it is not, for two reasons:

  1. The along-bore gravity component genuinely changes the bullet's speed, so drag and time of flight change too. That is why the app's uphill and downhill answers are not identical (uphill decelerates the bullet, slightly increasing time of flight and drop): at 600 yd and 30°, the app gives 88.16 in uphill versus 86.81 in downhill.
  2. The bullet spends a different amount of time in the air, so the drag history is different — and drag is not linear in anything.

4. Aerodynamic jump

What it is, and why it surprises people. This is the effect that catches almost every shooter out: a purely horizontal crosswind produces a vertical shift in impact.

The mechanism: as the bullet exits the muzzle into a crosswind, the wind immediately gives it a small angle of attack — the nose is no longer aligned with the air flow. The bullet is a gyroscope. A gyroscope responds to a torque by precessing 90° away from it. So a horizontal aerodynamic torque produces a vertical deflection. It happens in the first few feet of flight and then persists as a fixed angular offset for the entire trajectory.

The formula (Litz):

l = bulletLengthIn / bulletDiameterIn            # length in calibres

AJ_MOA_per_mph = 0.01·Sg − 0.0024·l + 0.032

aeroJumpMOA = −twistSign · AJ_MOA_per_mph · crosswindMph

Sg is the same Miller stability factor computed for spin drift. Because the result is an angle, it is applied as a constant MOA offset at every distance — which means in inches it grows linearly with range.

Sign convention: the jump is the crosswind vector rotated 90° in the direction of spin. For a right-hand twist, wind from the left jumps the impact DOWN; wind from the right jumps it UP. A left-hand twist reverses both.

Inputs required: the full spin description (twist, direction, length, diameter) plus bullet weight — because Sg is needed — and the crosswind component. Without spin data the effect is silently skipped.

Magnitude. For a 1:12 .308 with the 175 gr bullet (Sg ≈ 1.67, l ≈ 4.03 calibres), the formula gives 0.0390 MOA per mph. At 10 mph:

5. Powder temperature sensitivity

What it is. Smokeless powder burns faster when it is warm. A round left on a hot dashboard leaves the muzzle meaningfully faster than the same round out of a cold truck bed at dawn. Temperature-insensitive powders exist, but no powder is perfectly flat, and the sensitivity is a property of your specific load that you determine by chronographing at two temperatures.

The formula:

adjustedMV = muzzleVelocity + (actualTempF − referenceTempF) · fpsPerDegF

A straight linear correction. It is applied before the trajectory is solved — the corrected muzzle velocity is what the solver receives, so the effect propagates through drag, time of flight and every other calculation naturally rather than being patched on at the end.

Inputs required: all three of reference temperature, sensitivity in fps per °F, and the actual temperature. If any one is missing, the muzzle velocity passes through unchanged.

Magnitude: at a typical 1.0 fps/°F, a 40 °F swing is 40 fps.

6. Wind deflection

What it is. A crosswind does not blow the bullet sideways like a leaf. What actually happens is more subtle and more useful to understand: the bullet is slowed by drag along its path, and drag acts along the air-relative velocity. In a crosswind the air mass is moving, so the bullet's velocity relative to the air has a sideways component from the instant it leaves the muzzle, and drag pulls the bullet toward matching the air mass's motion.

The classical way to express this is Didion's lag rule: the deflection equals the wind speed multiplied by the bullet's lag time — the difference between its actual time of flight and the time a drag-free bullet would have taken.

deflection ≈ windSpeed · ( timeOfFlight − range/muzzleVelocity )

What the app does. It does not use that formula. The crosswind enters the integration directly, as part of the air-relative velocity in the drag calculation:

rz = vz − windSpeed

The sideways deflection then emerges from the physics for free, correct through transonic and at any air density. The test suite verifies that the result agrees with the Didion lag rule to within ±15 %, and that deflection is linear in wind speed.

Inputs required: a full-value (90°) crosswind speed in mph. Positive means wind from the left. The range card screen converts a logged clock-position wind into that full-value component:

crosswindMph = −windSpeed · sin(clockPosition · 30°)

so 9 o'clock (from the left) is positive, 3 o'clock is negative, and 12 or 6 o'clock is zero.

What the model does not do

Honesty about limits is part of a trustworthy solution. This is a point-mass 3-DOF solver, and there are real phenomena it does not represent:

  • It is not a 6-DOF model. The bullet has no modelled attitude — no pitch, no yaw, no angular momentum in the equations of motion. Spin drift and aerodynamic jump, which are genuinely attitude phenomena, are supplied as Litz's empirical closed-form corrections applied after the fact. They are good approximations, not derived physics.
  • Transonic yaw instability is not modelled. As a bullet decelerates through roughly Mach 1.2 down to Mach 0.9, the centre of pressure shifts abruptly and some bullets develop yaw that dramatically increases drag and dispersion. The reference .308 crosses Mach 1.2 at about 800 yd and Mach 1.0 at about 975 yd — so its last 200 yards are exactly the regime where the model is least trustworthy. The app will happily print a 1200 yd row for a .308; treat rows below about Mach 1.2 as indicative, and confirm them on steel.
  • Bullet-specific pitch and yaw damping are not modelled. Two bullets with identical BCs but different damping behaviour will be given identical trajectories.
  • Barrel harmonics, muzzle-exit conditions and vertical stringing are outside the model entirely. Positive compensation, node behaviour and ES-driven vertical dispersion are properties of your rifle and load, not of exterior ballistics. They are what your confirmed DOPE captures and the solver cannot.
  • Wind is a single constant full-value crosswind for the whole flight. There is no wind gradient with range, no vertical wind component, and the head/tailwind component is discarded when a clock-position wind is converted. Real wind varies along the flight path, and that variation is usually a larger error than anything else in this chapter.
  • The Eötvös vertical correction uses muzzle velocity, not the bullet's instantaneous speed. This is the standard closed-form treatment and the resulting error is small compared with the effect itself.
  • The altitude fallback supplies pressure only — no temperature lapse rate is applied. Always enter the actual temperature.
  • The app cannot detect a BC entered in the wrong drag model. See the table in the Drag section for how badly that goes.

The right way to think about it: this engine will get you a very good first-round solution — cross-validated to half an inch at 1000 yards — and the app's truing tools exist so that your confirmed DOPE, not the model, has the final word. Where prediction and observation disagree, the observation is right.

From your confirmed DOPE

This is the part that makes the app more than a calculator.

A DOPE entry marked confirmed does two things:

  1. It overrides the prediction at that distance. The card row shows your observed hold, marked as confirmed, with the prediction in small type underneath if the two disagree meaningfully. Nothing computed can beat something you actually saw.
  1. It becomes a calibration target. The truing routines search for the muzzle velocity, or the drag scale factor, that best reproduces all your confirmed holds at once — and that corrected solution then improves every row, including distances you have never fired.

Entries that are not marked confirmed are kept as history and are never used for either. That distinction is the whole reason the flag exists: a hold you dialled while guessing, or a shot you called bad, must not be allowed to bend the solution.

Two rules of thumb. Confirm holds at distance — a hold at 200 yd carries almost no information about drag, which is why velocity truing requires 300 yd and drag truing requires 400. And confirm them in conditions you actually recorded, because the app will compare your observed hold against a prediction made in the conditions on file.
